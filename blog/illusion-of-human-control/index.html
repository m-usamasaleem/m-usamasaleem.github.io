The Illusion of Human Control in the Age of AI

What if the biggest risk of AI is not that it replaces human decisions, but that it gradually changes how humans make them?

Muhammad Usama Saleem · November 2026 · 7 min read · 12 sources

Every tool gives something, and asks something

1970s

Pocket calculators

Fast, accurate arithmetic for everyone.

Mental arithmetic became optional.

We are living through an extraordinary race to adopt AI. Across industries, the conversation revolves around efficiency, productivity, automation, and competitive advantage. And whenever concerns about AI emerge, we often return to one reassuring principle: keep a human in the loop.

But I wonder whether we are asking the wrong question.

What if the human remains in control of the decision, but no longer independently forms the judgment behind it?

01From evaluating to confirming

Consider something simple. An AI system produces several excellent recommendations. Over time, we become familiar with its reliability. Reviewing its outputs starts to feel less necessary. Eventually, we may still approve every decision, but our role has subtly shifted from evaluating to confirming.

02This is not merely hypothetical

A 2025 CHI study involving 319 knowledge workers found that greater confidence in generative AI was associated with less reported critical-thinking effort.1 A 2024 Nature Human Behaviour meta-analysis of 106 experiments found that human-AI teams, on average, performed worse than whichever worked better alone, particularly on decision-making tasks.2

These findings challenge a comfortable assumption: placing a human at the end of an automated process does not necessarily preserve meaningful human judgment.

03How we got here

None of this began with generative AI. For four decades, research on automation has described the same pattern in cockpits, control rooms, and navigation. What is new is the scale: the same dynamic now reaches everyday cognitive work.

04Beyond the workplace

Human intelligence develops through struggle, disagreement, uncertainty, reflection, and social interaction. Our institutions and relationships depend on these processes too. A 2026 study in Science found that overly agreeable AI could increase people's certainty in their own judgment while reducing their willingness to repair interpersonal conflicts.3

AI does not need to make decisions for us to influence who we become as decision-makers.

05Performance is not capability

Yet most AI adoption strategies continue to measure what technology helps us accomplish, rather than what repeated dependence on that technology might change in us.

There is an important distinction between improving human performance and developing human capability. We can become more productive with AI without necessarily becoming more capable without it.

This does not mean we should resist AI. Its benefits are substantial and well measured,91011 and the evidence does not establish inevitable long-term cognitive decline. The challenge is to design systems that strengthen human capabilities rather than merely compensate for them.

06AI that makes us think harder

Perhaps, in certain consequential contexts, the next generation of AI should do more than provide increasingly convincing answers.

It should help us recognize uncertainty, examine assumptions, consider alternative perspectives, and understand who might be affected by our decisions. Sometimes, the most valuable AI interaction might be one that makes us think harder rather than one that makes thinking unnecessary. Designs that ask people to commit to a judgment before seeing the AI's have measurably reduced over-reliance, though users found them less pleasant.12

Not every task requires this kind of friction. But decisions involving learning, human relationships, professional responsibility, or other people's lives may deserve it.

The deeper question is not whether humans remain in the loop. It is whether the loop still requires humans to think.

We are investing enormous resources in making AI more intelligent.

Perhaps we should invest just as seriously in understanding what happens to human intelligence, judgment, and relationships when AI becomes an ordinary part of everyday life.

Because the long-term success of AI should not be measured only by how much it can do for us, but also by what it enables us to remain capable of doing ourselves.
