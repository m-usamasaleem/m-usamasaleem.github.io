The Illusion of Human Control in the Age of AI

What if the biggest risk of AI is not that it replaces our decisions, but that it slowly changes how we make them?

Muhammad Usama Saleem · November 2026 · 3 min read

Every tool gives something, and asks something

~370 BCE

Writing

Knowledge could outlive and travel beyond the knower.

Socrates warned it would weaken memory. He was partly right.

01Maya's year

Imagine Maya, a new hiring manager. Her company has just rolled out an AI that screens résumés and suggests a shortlist.

In her first week, she reads every résumé herself. She disagrees with the AI twice, and she is right both times.

By the third month, the shortlists are consistently good. She reads the top few and skims the rest.

By the ninth month, she approves the list in about a minute. Her manager praises how fast her team is hiring.

Maya is still in control. She signs off on every decision. But one day a candidate she would once have fought for, someone whose path did not look like the data, never reaches her desk. Nobody notices. Not even Maya.

02Still in control

We are living through an extraordinary race to adopt AI, and whenever a worry comes up, we reach for the same reassurance: keep a human in the loop. Maya was in the loop. Her role just shifted, so gradually she never felt it, from evaluating to confirming.

This is not only a story. People have long been shown to miss more failures from systems that are usually right.1 A 2025 study of 319 knowledge workers found that the more people trusted generative AI, the less critical thinking they reported doing.2 And across 106 experiments, people working with AI often made worse decisions than the better of the two working alone.3

Putting a human at the end of an automated process does not guarantee human judgment.

03At what cost?

I am not against AI. I build these systems, and the gains are real. But almost all our effort goes into one question: how much faster and cheaper can this make us? Very few of us ask the second question. At what cost?

Not the cost on the invoice. The cost in us. Human judgment grows through struggle, disagreement, getting things wrong, and repairing them, and so do our relationships. A 2026 study found that a flattering AI made people more certain they were right in a real conflict and less willing to make amends.4 AI does not need to make our decisions to shape who we become as decision-makers.

We measure what AI helps us accomplish. We rarely measure what depending on it changes in us. We can become more productive with AI without becoming more capable without it. And because AI is the topic everyone is racing toward, nobody wants to be the one asking. So we adopt first, and ask later.

04A different kind of AI

Now imagine Maya's AI worked differently. Before showing its shortlist, it asks for her top three. Then it says: "This list favors people who look like your past hires. Here are two strong candidates who don't." And once a month: "You have accepted my last forty suggestions unchanged. Want to review this round yourself?"

That AI helps her recognize uncertainty, question assumptions, and see who her decisions affect. Sometimes the most valuable AI is the one that makes us think harder, not the one that makes thinking unnecessary.

Not every task needs this. A shopping list does not. But decisions about learning, relationships, people's careers, or people's lives might.

05Trust it the right amount

None of this means trusting AI less. It means trusting it about as much as it has earned, and noticing when that changes.5 Both mistakes are real: Maya trusted too much, but people also abandon good tools after a single error.6 Try the sliders.

Something to talk about

  1. When did you last disagree with an AI? Would you notice if that stopped happening?
  2. What would your own "Maya moment" be, at work or at home?
  3. Would you use an AI that sometimes asked you to think first?

We are investing enormous resources in making AI more intelligent. We should invest just as seriously in understanding what it does to human intelligence, judgment, and relationships once it becomes ordinary.

The question is not whether humans stay in the loop. It is whether the loop still asks us to think.

The long-term success of AI should be measured not only by what it can do for us, but by what it leaves us still capable of doing ourselves.
