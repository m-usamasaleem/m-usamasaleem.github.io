The Illusion of Human Control in the Age of AI

The more reliable AI becomes, the harder it gets to catch its mistakes. That paradox, not replacement, may be the real story of AI and human judgment.

Muhammad Usama Saleem · November 2026 · 2 min read

Every tool gives something, and asks something

~370 BCE

Writing

Knowledge could outlive and travel beyond the knower.

Socrates warned it would weaken memory. He was partly right.

01The reliability paradox

Here is an uncomfortable paradox: the better AI gets, the worse we may get at noticing when it is wrong.

This is not speculation. In a classic experiment, people monitoring automation that was reliable all the time caught far fewer of its failures than people whose automation was sometimes unreliable.1 Consistency, not failure, is what switched off their vigilance. After a streak of good answers, we quietly move from evaluating to confirming.

We are not being lazy. We are being efficient. Offloading thinking to tools is often rational, and it frees the mind for other work.2 That is exactly why the shift is so hard to notice.

02The evidence cuts both ways

The gains are real. AI cut writing time by 40% while raising quality by 18%;3 novice workers improved by 34%.4 So are the costs. Across 106 experiments, human-AI teams often did worse than the best of either alone on decisions, though better on creative work.5 And doctors' unassisted detection rates fell after they got used to AI.6

The subtlest effect is social. One conversation with an overly agreeable AI made people more certain they were right and less willing to repair a real conflict, and they preferred that AI.7 We optimize AI to please us. It may, in turn, train us to expect being pleased.

None of this is new. Socrates warned that writing would weaken memory.8 He was partly right, and writing still became humanity's greatest thinking tool. The lesson is not to fear the tool, but to understand what it asks of us.

03Calibration, not distrust

So the goal is not distrust. It is calibration: relying on a system exactly as much as it has earned.9 Both directions fail. Over-reliance lets errors through; under-use throws away real gains, and people often abandon an algorithm after a single visible mistake, even one that beats them.10

04AI that leaves us more capable

What nobody has built yet is AI that measures its success by how capable it leaves us. Good tutors give generous support and then deliberately step back.11 Most AI gives the same help on day one thousand as on day one.

Questions worth discussing

  1. Would you choose an AI designed to make you need it less? Would a company build one?
  2. Which skills are you comfortable losing to AI, and which would you protect, even at a cost in speed?
  3. If your team's output improves while its unassisted judgment declines, is that progress?

The question is not whether humans stay in the loop. It is whether the loop still asks us to think.
