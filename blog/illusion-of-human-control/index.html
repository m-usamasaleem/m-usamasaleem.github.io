The Illusion of Human Control in the Age of AI

The risk is not that AI replaces our decisions. It is that it quietly changes how we make them.

Muhammad Usama Saleem · November 2026 · 1 min read

Every tool gives something, and asks something

~370 BCE

Writing

Knowledge could outlive and travel beyond the knower.

Socrates warned it would weaken memory. He was partly right.

"Keep a human in the loop" is our default safeguard. But after a streak of good answers, we stop evaluating and start confirming. Trust grows faster than it is checked.

The evidence cuts both ways. AI cut writing time by 40% and raised quality by 18%;1 novice workers gained 34%.2 Yet human-AI teams often underperform the best of either alone on decisions,3 and doctors' unassisted detection fell after AI exposure.4

The worry is old. Socrates feared writing would weaken memory. He was partly right, and writing still transformed us.5

So the goal is not distrust. It is calibration: relying on AI exactly as much as it deserves. Over-reliance lets errors through; under-use wastes real gains.67

What we have not built yet: AI that steps back as we grow, the way good tutors do;8 that tracks our capability, not just our productivity; and that offers the other side instead of simply agreeing.9

The question is not whether humans stay in the loop. It is whether the loop still asks us to think.
