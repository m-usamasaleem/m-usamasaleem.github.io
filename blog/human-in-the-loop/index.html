The Human Is in the Loop. Is the Loop Still in the Human?

Everywhere AI arrives, the same reassurance follows: keep a human in the loop. The research says that is necessary and nowhere near sufficient.

Muhammad Usama Saleem · November 2026 · 6 min read

Every industry adopting AI is converging on one safeguard. Medicine, hiring, education, law, finance: put a person between the model and the decision, and the risk is handled. I build these systems, and I believe in many of them. But "a human is in the loop" describes where a person sits in a workflow. It says nothing about whether that person is still doing the thinking.

Decades of research on automation, and a fast-growing body on generative AI, point to the same uncomfortable pattern. The human stays in the loop. The loop gradually stops needing the human.

01Trust is built by streaks, not by audits

Think about how trust actually forms. A tool gets it right three times, and the fourth time you check less carefully. By the sixth answer, if it makes a mistake, you are already inclined to accept it. That is not carelessness. It is how attention works.

Evidence

In a controlled study, users who saw an AI system's strengths first made significantly more errors from automation bias than users who saw its weaknesses first. Early success did not just build trust; it lowered vigilance for later mistakes.1 Human-factors research has shown for years that automation complacency affects novices and experts alike and does not go away with practice.2

So a person who reviews a hundred good AI outputs is not becoming a better reviewer. They are being trained, very effectively, to approve.

02The checker gets weaker

Human-in-the-loop assumes the human brings independent judgment to catch what the model misses. That judgment is a skill, and skills decay when they are not used.

Evidence

After AI-assisted polyp detection was introduced at four endoscopy centres, experienced endoscopists' detection rate on unassisted colonoscopies fell from 28.4% to 22.4%.3 In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking, while self-confidence was associated with more.4 Early EEG work on AI-assisted essay writing reports weaker neural engagement and lower recall of one's own text, though that study is small and preliminary.5

Lisanne Bainbridge named this in 1983: automating the routine work leaves operators with the hardest tasks and the least practice at them.6 Forty years later, generative AI is making the irony universal.

We are measuring how accurate the model is. We are not measuring what it is doing to the person checking it.

03In the loop is not the same as in control

When an automated system fails, someone has to absorb the blame. Madeleine Clare Elish calls the human operator a moral crumple zone: the person nominally in control, holding responsibility for a system whose behaviour they could not fully see or change.7

That is the quiet risk in "human in the loop." It can become a way of assigning accountability rather than preserving judgment. And beyond any one decision, there is a slower cost. Our relationships, institutions, and communities were built on people forming judgments, owning them, and learning from being wrong. If we outsource that habit one convenient task at a time, we may only notice what we lost long after it is gone.

04What consequence-aware AI could look like

None of this is an argument against adoption. In many fields AI is already genuinely valuable. It is an argument that the efficiency question, "will this make us faster?", needs a companion question: at what cost to the people using it? In high-stakes fields, I think AI systems should start answering that question themselves.

  1. Friction proportional to stakes. For consequential decisions, ask the person to commit to their own judgment before revealing the AI's. "Cognitive forcing" designs like this measurably reduce overreliance. Users rate them least favourably, which is exactly why they will not appear without deliberate design.8
  2. Reliance made visible. Show people their own patterns: how often they accept suggestions unchanged, and how often the AI was wrong when they did. Trust should be calibrated against evidence, not built by streaks.
  3. Skill preservation by design. Aviation already does this. The FAA encourages airlines to keep pilots flying manually, because constant automation erodes the skills needed when it fails.9 Clinicians, analysts, and engineers deserve the same deliberate practice.
  4. Consequences stated up front. Before acting, a system could say what it is uncertain about, who is affected, and what a person should independently verify. Not a disclaimer, but a short, specific prompt to think.
  5. Measure the human, not just the model. Every deployment reports model accuracy. Few report whether human accuracy without the AI is holding steady. That second number is the one that tells us if the loop still has a human in it.

Adopt AI where it adds value. But treat human judgment as something the system must protect, not just a checkbox it must include. Keeping the human in the loop only matters if we also keep the loop in the human.
